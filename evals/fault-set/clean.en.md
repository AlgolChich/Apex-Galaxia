The hangar smelled of oil and wet concrete. The lamp over the bench buzzed each time the wind pushed at the roof.

"You alone?" she said.

"Second truck never came." He laid the wrench down beside the others.

She did not answer. She took the pot off the stove, smelled it, put it back. Past the fence a dog began to bark and stopped as if someone had put a hand on it.

"Coffee's burnt," she said.

He wiped his hands on a rag that had been white a long time ago and looked at the door again. The radio on the shelf gave out a sentence of static and a woman's voice that was not hers, reading numbers. He turned the dial a hair to the left; the numbers came back clearer, and he wrote them on the rag.

By the time the headlights came up the road there were three rows of numbers on the rag and the pot had gone cold. He put the wrench in his pocket, where it did not fit, and stood up to see whose truck it was.
