The ticket book said ten. June counted the saloon again, from the tea urn to the door, and got eleven.

She did it a third time with the punch in her hand, touching each head with her eyes the way her mother touched jars on a shelf. Two fishermen asleep against each other. The schoolteacher with her marking. The boy from the garage and his dog. A couple she did not know, sharing one coat. The man with the car on the deck, who kept going out to look at it. That was nine, and the tenth was the old woman by the window, and the eleventh was the old woman by the window too, because June had counted her once as a passenger and once as a problem.

The old woman had a cardboard suitcase on her knees and both hands flat on top of it. Her ticket, when June asked for it, was dry, which no ticket on this boat had been for a month.

"That's tomorrow," June said.

"Is it." The woman looked at the date as if it were a weather report. "I bought it yesterday."

"There's no boat tomorrow. This is the last one."

"Then it's lucky I caught it."

June punched the ticket anyway, because the punch was in her hand, and the hole came out clean. She went up to the wheelhouse with the book.

Halloran had the thermos between his knees and had not opened it. The strait was the color of nothing, and the sound of it against the hull was the only thing in the room that moved.

"Eleven," June said.

"Ten."

"Eleven. Window seat. Ticket's for tomorrow."

He did not turn around. His hands were steady on the wheel, which was the only place they were ever steady.

"Get her off," he said.

"We're forty minutes out."

"Then get her off at the other end."

"That's where she's going, Cap."

The engine changed its note under them, the way it did when the current took the bow. Halloran reached for the thermos, looked at it, put it back. On the door behind June the company rule was painted in letters that someone had retouched every spring for thirty years.

"We don't stop in the strait," he said, to the glass.

June went back down. The old woman had not moved. The suitcase had a luggage tag, and the name on the tag was one June had seen before, on a brass plate in the saloon, under the words *lost in the strait*.
