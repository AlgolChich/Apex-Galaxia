The hangar smelled of oil and wet concrete, and the lamp over the bench buzzed each time the wind pushed at the roof. He laid the wrench down beside the others and wiped his hands on a rag that had been white a long time ago, then looked at the door again.

She had told him to wait until the second truck came, and the second truck had not come, and the coffee on the stove had gone from bitter to burnt while he listened to the roof and counted the gaps between the gusts that rattled the panels.

Somewhere past the fence a dog began to bark and stopped as if someone had put a hand on it. He picked the wrench up again because his hands wanted something, and he turned the bolt on the bench a quarter turn for no reason he could have named.

The radio on the shelf gave out a sentence of static and a woman's voice that was not hers, reading numbers, and then the static again. He turned the dial a hair to the left and the numbers came back clearer, and he wrote them on the rag without knowing why.

By the time the headlights came up the road he had written three rows of numbers on the rag and the coffee had gone cold in the pot, and he put the wrench in his pocket, where it did not fit, and stood up to see whose truck it was.
